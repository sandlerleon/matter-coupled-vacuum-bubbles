Can a declining dark-matter density switch on the decay of a vacuum? For a scalar whose coupling to dark matter is centered on a false vacuum, the mean-field effective potential becomes metastable below a degeneracy density, and Coleman–De Luccia tunneling switches on. We test the assumption behind this picture, that the field feels a smooth density. For a vacuum at the dark-energy scale, the field's Compton length is approximately 0.07 mm, and the dark-matter energy inside a Compton volume is only 0.05 mφ⁴. Spatially resolved calculations (static fields, first-order rates and nonperturbative bounces around discrete sources) indicate that discrete sources suppress the decay far less than the mean field predicts: for 1 MeV particles, a fraction 7 × 10⁻⁸ of the mean-field suppression survives. Within this model, particle dark matter leaves the vacuum close to its zero-density rate; general many-body configurations remain open. The mean field holds for wave dark matter between approximately 3 meV and 0.25 eV, which a 1+1-dimensional wall simulation confirms. The decay is then environmental: a region completes its transition at a scale factor proportional to (1+δ)^(1/3), voids first, while galactic halos keep the held vacuum. The walls run away; the dark matter crossing them gains the sudden-crossing energy and trails the wall at 0.75c. Transitioned regions recollapse, and an isolated Hubble-scale bubble develops a trapped surface before its interior bounces. An optional neutrino coupling is limited by an approximate early-universe criterion to y ≲ 10⁻⁴, with no observable consequence.
